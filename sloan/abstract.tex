\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries R-Value: Pricing NBA Production Against True Team Expenditure}
\end{center}
\vspace{2pt}

\textbf{Introduction.} Public player-value metrics evaluate production but fail
to take the associated costs into account. Nominal salary greatly underestimates
those costs: under the luxury tax, a dollar paid by a repeater taxpayer can cost
the franchise several times one paid by a team below the tax line. We ask which
players and teams achieve the highest production per dollar of true expenditure,
and whether cost-efficiency is connected to winning championships. The question
is timely, since the apron system introduced in the 2023 CBA was specifically
intended to curb high-spending rosters.

\textbf{Method.} R-Value matches a performance core with a CBA-correct cost
model. The core combines five z-scored components $z_k$---box production (rate
and volume), usage-scaled shooting efficiency, minute-shrunk on-court offensive
and defensive impact, and PIE---into a composite index, applies the normal
cumulative distribution function $\Phi$ to it, and adjusts for availability over
$m$ minutes:
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{CPI}=\sum_{k=1}^{5} w_k z_k,
\qquad
\mathrm{PS}=100\,\Phi(\mathrm{CPI}),
\qquad
\mathrm{WPS}=\mathrm{PS}\bigl(0.55+0.45A\bigr),
\quad A=\sqrt{\tfrac{\min(m,\,2200)}{2200}}.
\end{equation*}
The weights $w_k$ come from a two-stage simplex grid search maximising All-NBA
recall@15 and team-win rank correlation. The cost model computes each season's
luxury tax $T(P)$ on payroll $P$ from the bracket schedule then in effect (2017
CBA through 2022--23, the 2023 CBA thereafter), applies repeater rates, and
grosses up each salary $s_i$ by the team's total-expenditure multiplier:
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{TE}=P+T(P),
\qquad
\mathrm{CI}_i=\frac{s_i\,(\mathrm{TE}/P)}{\overline{\mathrm{TE}}},
\qquad
R_i=100\cdot\operatorname{norm}\!\left[\mathrm{WPS}_i
\left(\frac{\overline{\mathrm{CI}}}{\mathrm{CI}_i}\right)^{0.35}\right],
\end{equation*}
normalised so the league average is 100. Team R-Value divides minute-weighted
output by total expenditure. Data covers six seasons---2018--19 and 2021--22
through 2025--26---excluding the COVID-disrupted 2019--20 and 2020--21, whose
irregular schedules and pro-rated tax settlements undermine the availability and
cost models respectively.

\textbf{Results.} Under leave-one-season-out cross-validation---each parameter
refitted while the season being scored is excluded---the core recovers 78.9\% of
All-NBA selections in its top 15, against 71.1\% for PIE$\times$minutes, 34.4\%
for raw plus-minus and 23.3\% for estimated net rating, and correlates with team
wins at $\rho = 0.914$ (Table~1). Weights are stable: four of six folds select
the same vector, the offensive-impact weight identical in all six.

Championships are generally not obtained efficiently (Figure~1). Among the six
champions, production-per-dollar ranked 16th, 29th, 14th, 13th, 3rd and 14th of
30. The 2021--22 Warriors won at an R-Value of 49, on a \$179M payroll carrying
a \$191M repeater tax. A fitted title score weights eight-man rotation quality
roughly nine times the top-two star premium, and ranks actual champions 2.83rd
of 30 out of sample. Across 2025--26's 582 players, R-Value is positively skewed
at $+1.04$ (median 94.9 against a mean fixed at 100): most contracts yield
below-average value per dollar, while a few rookie-level agreements return
multiples of it.

\textbf{Conclusion.} The rosters offering the best cost value are seldom the
ones that go on to win titles; instead, teams achieve competitiveness by
spending through these short value windows. R-Value captures that trade-off in
CBA-accurate dollars, letting front offices assess roster decisions on actual
expenditure rather than nominal salary, and ranks players, evaluates teams and
forecasts seasons from one model.
